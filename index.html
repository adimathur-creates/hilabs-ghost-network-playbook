Ghosts in the directory: a 30-day forward-deployed playbook for finding providers who aren't really there

Federal auditors found that on average 55% of the behavioral-health providers listed by Medicare Advantage and Medicaid plans had seen no members of that plan all year. The problem

Directories list providers who left, never see the plan's members, or only do telehealth. Members call, can't get care, and regulators now count that against the plan.

The insight

The plan already holds the best evidence: its own claims. A provider with no claims in 12 months is the first place to look, and the cheapest one to check.

The trap

Remove every ghost at once and some counties fall below network adequacy minimums. Fixing the directory and fixing the network have to happen together.

2. The problem statement

A new health plan client has a directory it can't trust, an audit coming, and no idea which of its listings are ghosts. In 30 days, find the listings most likely to be wrong using the plan's own claims, verify them in the order that protects members most, and fix the directory within the 90-day and 2-business-day rules, without removing so many providers that the plan falls below network adequacy minimums in any county.

3. Client discovery: the first two weeks

Who I'd meet, and what I need from each
  • Provider data ops: how directory changes flow today, backlog size, where rosters come from and how late
  • Network management: which counties and specialties are already thin; recruiting pipeline
  • Compliance: last CMS or state audit findings; dates of the next ones; complaint log
  • Claims / IT: claims extract with rendering NPI, service location, place of service, date; claims lag
  • Member services: calls about providers who couldn't be reached
Questions that change the design
  • Do claims carry the rendering NPI, or only the billing group's? (If only the group's, the method needs roster data to work.)
  • How many months of claims run-out before the data is complete?
  • Which providers are delegated (a group manages their data)?
  • Is telehealth listed separately from in-person locations?
  • Who signs off on removing a listing, and how fast?

5. The adequacy trap, and how to handle it

Query 3 is the one a client will thank you for. A ghost listing is wrong, but it's also counted toward the plan's network adequacy. Remove it without a plan and a county can fall below the minimum, which is its own compliance problem. So every inactive listing goes into one of three lanes:

1

County still adequate without it

Verify (call, or check recent roster data) and remove or correct within 2 business days of confirming.

2

County would fall below the minimum

Verify first and flag to network management the same week. Correct the listing (members shouldn't be sent to a ghost) and start recruiting in parallel, so the compliance team sees both moves together.

3

Not a ghost after all

New providers, part-timers, telehealth-only, or claims billed under a group NPI. Fix the listing's details (telehealth flag, hours, group) and add the pattern to the rules so it isn't flagged again.

7. What's product, and what's this client

Build once (product)
  • Inactive-listing detection from claims, with the 6-month new-provider exclusion
  • Adequacy impact report (inactive vs. minimums, by county and specialty)
  • Priority score for outreach, feeding the voice agent's call list
  • Before/after accuracy measured on a random verified sample
Configure per client
  • Specialty taxonomy mapping
  • Claims lag and look-back window
  • Rendering vs. billing NPI handling; delegated group rules
  • The plan's own adequacy standards and sign-off workflow

The rule I'd hold to: if the same client-specific fix shows up at a second client, it moves into the product.

8. Metrics

  • For the client: directory accuracy on a random verified sample (the only honest measure); share of listings verified in the last 90 days; median days from a confirmed change to the directory update (target: 2 business days or less); share of behavioral-health listings with claims in the last 12 months.
  • For the network: county–specialty pairs below the minimum, before and after; recruiting started for each gap.
  • For HiLabs: time to first value (days from data received to a baseline the client accepts); cost per verified listing; share of client-specific fixes later moved into the product.
  • Guardrail: listings wrongly removed (providers who were active) found in the verified sample. This should be near zero; a wrong removal sends a member away from a real doctor.

9. Risks and what would make this wrong

  • Group billing. If claims carry only the group's NPI, individual providers look inactive. Check the rendering NPI fill rate on day one.
  • Claims lag. Recent months are incomplete. Use a window that ends before the lag, or count only claims older than the run-out.
  • Low-volume specialists. Some real providers see few of this plan's members. "No claims" means "check first", never "remove automatically".
  • Data protection. Claims are PHI. Minimum necessary fields, the client's environment or an approved secure one, and access logged.
  • The synthetic data is mine. The method follows the OIG's; the numbers in the lab are illustrations, not findings.
